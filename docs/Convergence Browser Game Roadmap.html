Where the project stands

A React + TypeScript card duel with a real, deterministic rules engine, the full Convergence roster drawn live from its own card data, a juiced board with particle FX and a hand-built audio layer — and, since this pass, a practice opponent so it can be played alone.

What the build has

  • The engine. Pure functions over a serialisable state: legal-action generation, mana, 5 board slots, simultaneous combat with full retaliation, Taunt / Divine Shield / Freeze / Silence / Chained / Invulnerable, undo, cheat mode, and a seeded generator so every roll is reproducible but nothing is predictable.
  • Real choices. 44 effects stop and ask their controller — point at a minion, pick a card out of the enemy's hand, or choose a value.
  • Hero Powers. The player chooses one unlocked power before the duel. The bot receives one random power from all ten. It costs 2 mana and works once per turn.
  • Ascension Relics. A working equipment system: 27 relics shuffled into the shared deck, drawn into hand, paid for, worn two at a time, and dying with their bearer.
  • An opponent. A practice bot plays Player Two, including draw picks and targeting prompts, so the game can be played alone.
  • Hearthstone-style face attacks. Any ready minion may hit the enemy core unless a Taunt blocks the way, so a card's ATK finally reaches the thing that ends the game. There is no passive damage of any kind; cores start at 75. Mana still climbs a plain +1 a turn — duels simply run long enough that the ten-cost Greats reach the board.
  • Battlecry over Ongoing. 70 effects fire once on arrival and only 18 still repeat every turn, which are the growth-and-sustain engines that are meant to. A further 58 are Passive — continuously true while their minion lives, never triggering a second time — and 12 fire on death.
  • A blind draw, with one exception. One card a turn, no choice — unless Detective L is on your board, whose Foresight shows you two and lets you keep one.
  • The presentation. Parchment battlefield in a wooden frame, fanned hand, hover previews, pointer drag & drop with a curved targeting arrow, particle impacts diffed from state, death dissolves, screen shake, turn banners, a victory curtain call.
  • The audio. Convolution reverb on every voice, layered impacts, inharmonic FM metal, taiko under big plays, four escalating rarity fanfares, and a generated lofi bed looped seamlessly by folding its tail over its head.
  • The art. All 178 minion faces plus 27 relic faces, web-optimised from the 1500×2100 print masters, rendered full-bleed on every card.
  • Tests. Focused tests cover combat, card timing, targeting, randomness, Relics, Hero Powers, pacing, bot behavior, and saved-state invariants. A separate self-play harness fuzzes the engine with random legal moves, while effect coverage checks keep the roster tied to the engine.

What it still needs — the short version

  • Nothing, for the first time. The last gap was two licensed characters that printed no effect and no keyword at all. Both are done: Ultron Prime is a 6/3 Taunt whose Deathrattle replaces him with Vision, another 6/3 Taunt, and Gordon Freeman is a 1/3 whose passive gives every friendly minion Charge. The current roster has no unfinished named character effects; Basic reference cards may remain plain.

Everything else that was once on this list is either built or parked on purpose. Meta-progression exists now; fine balance is deliberately retired until a larger audience would notice it. Full detail on the Roadmap tab.

Version 1.0 — complete

Finished and shipped, 21 August 2026. It looks like a finished product and it plays like one: it has been played by a human, and it was fun. A match is good, and now it is also remembered — the result goes into a record, and every card you meet is marked in the gallery.

178 minion cards and 27 relics, none blank, every one carrying real artwork and its own theme. Three opponent levels. A duel runs about eleven turns each, the same shape as the games it sits beside. What is left is maintenance, not a missing piece.

How it actually plays

Not the design document — the rules as the engine really enforces them today.

The turn

  • You draw one card from the shared deck, straight into hand — no choice, exactly as in Hearthstone. Both players draw from the same deck, so the pool is contested.
  • Mana refills and grows by 1 a turn, capped at 10.
  • Opening hands are 3 cards each, and the player going second also keeps The Coin.
  • Both players are offered 2 Hero Powers at the opening and keep one. It costs 2 mana and can be used once on each of its owner's turns.
  • Frozen minions thaw, chains weaken, and your Ongoing engines fire.
  • Nothing happens passively to a core. Damage comes from swings and effects only.
  • You play cards, attack, and end the turn.

Combat

  • Simultaneous. The defender always hits back, even when the blow kills it.
  • Any ready minion may attack the enemy core, exactly as in Hearthstone — a defended board is no longer a wall. Attacking the core deals the attacker's ATK and takes no retaliation.
  • Taunt stops it. A Taunt minion (or Kojiro Sasaki soaking his side) must be attacked first, and closes the core until it is gone. Shinigami Eyes walks past both.
  • Divine Shield eats one instance of damage.
  • A minion with 0 ATK cannot attack at all.
  • Minions are asleep the turn they land, and attack once a turn unless their effect says otherwise.

The core clock

One clock, and it counts power: a ready minion swings at the enemy core for its full ATK unless a Taunt blocks the way. The current duel starts each core at 75 health, giving the ten-cost Greats room to reach the board without bending the mana curve. An accelerated mana ramp was tried instead and reverted: it skipped mana values 3 and 7, which quietly left every card costed at 3 or 7 with no on-curve turn. Move the dials and see how long a board needs.

Foresight — the draw choice is now a card, not a rule

Every turn used to open with a pick-1-of-2 draw. That is Hearthstone's blind draw now — but the mechanic was not deleted, it was given to a card:

Detective L (Legendary, 1 mana, Death Note) — Ongoing: while it lives, your draw shows you two cards and you keep one. The other goes to the bottom of the deck.

  • Why this card. Its printed text is already “Draw 1 card” and its printed flavour is “Justice works in probabilities.” L is the deduction-under-uncertainty character; turning a blind draw into a choice between two probabilities is the smallest possible stretch of any card in the roster.
  • How it behaves. Checked at the moment of drawing, so it works the turn it lands, dies with the minion, and a silenced or Chained Detective L gives nothing.
  • The open question: it is a 1-mana card carrying a permanent card-selection engine. Nothing else in the game filters draws. Pricing is deliberately untouched until the playtest — but if one card is going to be over-costed after it, this is the one.

Ongoing effects

15 of 178 cards are ongoing: they fire at the start of every one of your turns, forever. That convention still shapes the game — a board that survives compounds — but it is no longer the roster's centre of gravity. Most of the repeating text was re-read and re-labelled as Passive, which is continuously true rather than a fresh trigger each turn, and two of the 18 now remove something instead of only growing (Time Bomb clears the board, Rimuru eats a minion). The list is on the Roadmap tab.

Relics in play

Relics are shuffled into the shared deck like any other card. You draw one, pay its mana cost, and play it onto a friendly minion, where it stays until that minion dies. A minion can wear two, shown as gold badges in the corner of its card, and a movable relic can be passed to another friendly minion once a turn. Full system on the Relics tab.

Ascension Relics

Every relic's printed text talks about “the bearer”, so relics are equipment, not hero trinkets. That one decision made the whole system fall out.

How they work

  • All 27 relics are shuffled into the shared deck, alongside the 178 minions. Each exists once per game, and either player can draw it.
  • A relic is a card you pay for. Costs run from 1 (Elder wand, Allspark Cube and three more) to 5 (The Holy Grail). You play it onto a friendly minion with a free relic slot.
  • Two relics per minion. The two slots are independent: a full bearer cannot take a third, and moving one does not move or re-fire the other.
  • An effect that says gain an Ascension Relic pulls the next relic out of the draw pile and puts it in your hand, so it still has to be paid for and placed.
  • A relic dies with its bearer. That is the whole cost of the system — it goes back to nobody, not the deck, not the hand.
  • Some fire once on equip (doubling stats, healing, shielding); the rest are continuous and are read by combat, damage, disabling and the turn-start upkeep.
  • One Ring was re-cut when the passive ping was removed — its old text tripled a ping that no longer exists. It now adds 3 to its bearer's swing at the enemy core.

What it unlocked

  • Six minions whose entire printed effect is “gain an Ascension Relic” became real cards instead of a counter nobody could spend.
  • Ten Commandments tears a relic off an enemy minion and wears it.
  • Doctor Octopus destroys one outright.
  • Godrick's on-kill relic reward now actually rewards something.

Design calls made here: relics as equipment worn by a minion rather than trinkets held by a player, two per minion, destroyed with the bearer, and drawn from the one shared deck rather than a separate pool. All reversible — the hooks live in one place in the engine.

What is left, in the order worth doing it

Ordered by what actually improves the game, not by what is easiest to tick off. Everything here was read out of the code, not guessed.

done  A duel now leaves something behind

The duel itself was answered a while ago: it has been played by a human and it was fun. What that playtest exposed was not inside the match, it was around it. A duel ended and nothing survived it — no record, no rank, no reason your tenth duel differed from your first.

Two things now persist, both on this device, in their own storage separate from the save (a save holds one duel and is cleared when it ends; a record has to survive exactly that):

  • A record. Duels played, won and lost against each opponent level, an overall win rate, and your last ten results with the turn count of each. Reached from the title screen, which only offers the door once you have finished a duel.
  • A collection. Every one of the 205 cards and relics is marked in the gallery by how far it has got with you: dimmed until it has been in your hand, full colour once it has, a teal ring once you have played it, and a gold ring once you have won a duel with it on the board. One match deals you roughly 25 to 30 cards, so the roster is a long game.

Two larger ideas were weighed and deliberately not built. A ladder that locks Veteran and Ascendant behind wins would take two thirds of the game away from a player who wants it now, and the record already answers "how am I doing". A run, and the draft mode that pairs with it, is a bigger problem than it looks: this game deals both players from one shared deck, and that is exactly what gives Foresight and Clairvoyance their bite — the card the Ascendant throws away is the card you were about to draw. Give each player their own deck and that whole layer disappears.

parked  Fine balance — deliberately left where it is

Owner decision, 20 August 2026: this is retired for now, not abandoned. It comes back only if the game is ever put in front of a larger audience. The reasoning is worth stating plainly, because it is a judgement rather than an oversight. Pulling a first-player rate from 57% toward 50%, or a single card from +17 above its tier back to the middle, only matters once enough people play that anyone can tell. One person and a few friends is not that. Meanwhile the measurement costs 1 hour 40 minutes a run, roughly 91% of it the Ascendant ladder, and by its own statistics it cannot resolve a change smaller than about four points. The numbers below are kept because they are true and useful reading, not because they are a to-do list.

The last full measurement ran on 15 August 2026 over 1,000 duels (npm run check:balance). All eleven gate checks passed:

  • Does it end? Yes. Zero stalls, zero soft-locks, zero draws. Median duel 22 turns, about eleven each.
  • Is it a coin flip? Not quite, and this is the number to watch. The player going first wins 55.8%, against a pass band that stops at 56%. It passed by two tenths of a point.
  • Is it decided early? No. Whoever is clearly ahead at turn 5 wins only 56.6%. The duel tightens gradually instead of ending in its opening.
  • Was turn 1–3 dead time? It was, and it has been fixed. Openings that cannot legally play a card through three turns are down to 13.5%, against an 18% ceiling. Opening hands went from 1 and 2 up to 3 each, with The Coin still going to the second player.

Why it has to run again. After that measurement, the lore-tier pass changed the cost or the stats of 34 cards, and 3 cards were added that have never been measured at all (Mothership, Planetary Defense Grid, Black Hole). A card's balance verdict is a comparison against the average of its own cost bracket, so moving 34 cards moved the brackets underneath every card that shares them. The green board above is honest about the roster it measured and is no longer a description of the roster being played.

done  Price the roster for its new shape

The cards were costed when everything repeated forever. Now 70 of them are Battlecries worth exactly one use, and only 18 still generate every turn — so the two groups are mispriced in opposite directions. The Battlecries are too expensive for a single use, and the surviving engines are too cheap for an effect with no ceiling.

Pass 3 is done — 23 cards, every number measured against that card's own mana cost rather than the roster average. Mana cost was not touched, and never is. The interesting part was what the measurement caught that a reading of the cards would not have:

  • Two earlier "fixes" had backfired. APR was buffed in pass 1 off a 33.8% win rate and came back at 73.9%, the worst outlier in the game. Bigfoot was given 5 HP in pass 1 and did not move at all — because its keyword makes it unable to attack and it has no Taunt, so nothing obliged the enemy to hit it either. Its body was never the lever. It can swing now.
  • The three alignment anthems — Walter White, Kagaya, Flowey — were the biggest cluster in the roster at 16 to 18 points above their bracket. All three now buff everyone except themselves. They stay anthems and stop being beaters.
  • Margit's real cost was invisible on the card. The text said +3/+2; the code also healed the target to full every turn, so chip damage never stuck to an ever-growing minion.
  • Time Bomb had the same 0-attack, no-Taunt problem pass 1 fixed on four other cards and missed on this one. It was played 76.8% of the time it was drawn and still lost.

Pass 4 followed, 17 cards, and was deliberately gentler because the gaps were smaller. The roster went 36 → 19 → 14 outliers across the two passes, and five whole cost tiers are now completely clean — costs 2, 7, 8, 9 and 10 have no card measurably out of line with its peers. Blowouts fell 5.6% → 2.8%, and the first player now wins 50.2%.

Pass 5 is done, 16 cards, and it is the one that finally turned the whole board green. Cards more than nine points off their own cost bracket went 19 → 9; more than twelve points off, 8 → 4; and the gap between the best and worst card in the roster closed from 37.8 to 32.1 points. Fifteen of the sixteen landed inside ±9 and most inside ±4 — Thanos 30.2 → 41.8, Doomsday 32.2 → 41.3, One-Eyed Owl 68.0 → 53.0, Giant Tree 63.7 → 51.6.

All eleven gate checks passed in one run for the first time, bot ladder included: the hard bot beats easy 81.3% and normal 81.3%, normal beats easy 60.5%, first player wins 51.7%, median duel 21 turns, zero stalls, zero soft-locks, zero draws.

Detective L was buffed after all, reversing pass 4. That pass left him alone because the practice opponent could not value a drawn card, so his number meant nothing. It was true when written and no longer true when acted on — the bot fix that followed gave it a per-card hand valuation, and re-testing it directly showed it takes the stronger of two offered cards either way round. What the re-test really found was stranger: across 40 machine duels his Foresight opened once. He has to be drawn, played, and still alive at the next draw out of a shared 177-card deck, so his text happens in about one duel in ten and cannot move a win rate. His 35.1% was simply the win rate of a 1/1 body. He is a 2/3 now and sits 0.3 points off his bracket.

Passes 6 and 7 followed and took the nine cards pass 5 had exposed. Seven of the nine landed straight away — Gums 66.7 → 54.5, Joker 60.7 → 52.3, Aladdin Lamp 36.4 → 52.8, Morpheus 34.9 → 42.7, Ouken 38.8 → 54.8, Fort 60.1 → 52.7 — and pass 7 corrected the two that did not. Across all three passes the gap between the best and worst card in the roster closed from 37.8 to 28.4 points, cards badly out of line with their price went 19 → 8, and cards very badly out went 8 → 2. The gate has stayed green on all eleven checks throughout.

Two mistakes worth keeping, because both cost a whole run to find. Meruem was buffed +3/+2 in one step off −15.7 and came back 27.9 points higher at +11 — a big one-step buff on a badly losing card overshoots, which pass 3 had already learned once. And Eye of Sauron was nerfed and its gap got worse: its own win rate barely moved, but Fort was cut in the same pass, so the whole five-cost average fell underneath it. A gap is a difference between two numbers and either one of them can be the thing that moved.

The outlier count is not a score while a pass is running. It went 9 → 14 across pass 6 while the roster was getting tighter, because moving nine cards moves their price brackets’ averages and re-labels their neighbours. The spread between best and worst is the honest measure; the count is a worklist.

Two cards were deliberately left alone, which in a late pass matters as much as the changes. APR has been changed in two consecutive passes and pass 1 demonstrably overshot in the other direction, so a third cut is how a card gets destroyed. Detective L sits 12 points low, and the reason recorded here for leaving him alone has since been checked and found wrong. The note said the bot has no way to value drawing a card, so his effect was worth zero in every simulated duel. It was measured directly: given a choice between the weakest card in the game and the strongest, the bot takes the strongest, in either presentation order. It has priced a card in its own hand ever since the bot fix below landed.

What the measurement actually found is smaller and more awkward: across 40 bot-versus-bot duels his Foresight opened once. He has to be drawn, played, and still alive at the next draw, out of a shared 177-card deck — so his effect fires in roughly one duel in ten and cannot move a win rate built from a hundred and fifty of them. His 35.1% is very nearly the win rate of a 1/1 body. He is not unmeasurable; he is a card whose text almost never happens. Still not a card to buff blind — but the reason is now the right one.

His printed text was also wrong, and that has been fixed. The card read “Passive: Draw 1 card”, which is what every card in the game does anyway; it now reads “Passive: Your draw shows two cards. Take one.”

done  Relics are a decision now, not just weather

Relics still arrive by luck, but they no longer sit wherever luck dropped them. You can pass a relic to another of your minions once a turn — click the relic on the minion wearing it, then click the minion to give it to. Pull it off something about to die, or move it onto the threat that is about to swing. The five relics that spend themselves the instant they are equipped cannot be passed on, because re-equipping one would fire it again every turn.

And the ◈ Relics button opens the shelf: everything still in the rift, in the order it will be claimed, plus who is wearing what. No rule changed — but seeing what is coming turns the next relic from weather into something you can plan a turn around.

not doing  The mode that would make the roster matter

One shared deck, one card drawn a turn, duels running a median of 22 turns — a match shows roughly 25–30 of 205 cards, and the player chooses none of them. A draft or deckbuilding mode is the single change that would make the roster size mean something, and the engine already carries everything it needs: a card pool, a shuffler, and a state that serialises cleanly.

It also pairs with item 1. A drafted deck is the most natural thing for a run to carry forward, so building either one makes the other cheaper.

4  Deepen the relic system further

Moving a relic, wearing two at once, and seeing the shelf are all in. Choosing a relic is now partly in as well: Gol D. Roger offers 1 of 3 and Indiana Jones discovers 1 of 3, both straight into hand.

What is still open is the general case — every relic grant offering a choice, rather than two cards offering it. That is the hardest piece, because a relic can be claimed in the middle of a combat resolution (Godrick's kill grants one) and the engine has no way to stop and ask a question there; it can only pause inside effect resolution. Doing it properly means either teaching combat to suspend, or accepting that the choice appears for some grants and not others, which is worse than no choice at all.

Tesseract, once benched for needing a "move a minion to another slot" action the game does not have, was re-cut: its bearer now takes no retaliation when it attacks.

5  Smaller things, once the above is settled

  • An even stronger bot. It now sees Passive cards, counts multi-attackers properly and compares the cards in its own hand (see the done entry below). What it still does not do is plan a combo across turns, or hold a card back for a better moment next turn. It also thinks on the page's own thread, so the strongest level pauses the screen while it searches.

done  The bot could not see a fifth of your roster

The practice opponent decides what to play by scoring the board that each possible move would leave behind. That score was a checklist — Taunt, Divine Shield, is it frozen, can it attack — and “Passive” was not on it at all. Not scored low: absent. So 34 of your 177 cards were judged as bare bodies with their text deleted, and the only thing it knew about a hand was how many cards were in it, which made Detective L's entire purpose — choosing the better of two cards — worth precisely nothing.

That was never only a weak-opponent problem. The balance numbers come from these duels, so a Passive card's measured win rate was its body's win rate with the card text thrown away. Seven of the nineteen cards examined in pass 4 carried that warning.

All three holes are fixed: every Passive class is now priced (wards scale with health, conditional invulnerability is checked live so a broken condition is worth nothing, growth engines earn what an Ongoing earns), Flash's three swings and Vergil's two finally count toward lethal instead of one each, and the bot compares the cards in its own hand rather than counting them.

Seeing your hand was ruled out here as cheating, and that ruling has since been reversed on purpose. The three difficulties now differ in what the opponent is allowed to know, not only in how far it searches: Recruit and Veteran lost the accidental dice-reading they always had, and Ascendant openly reads your hand, the next draws, and the dice. The table of exactly which cheat belongs to which level is in the project README.md, generated against the engine so the two cannot disagree.

The subtle one, worth knowing: a card that punishes whoever attacks it must be priced on the attacker, never on itself. Charging it twice made the bot want to kill such minions more eagerly, because a valuable enemy is an attractive target. A test caught it.

done  The hotseat curtain, and a machine that plays the game for you

Two things that used to sit on the "later" list are built. The hotseat privacy curtain is in, so passing the laptop between two people no longer shows the incoming player the outgoing player's hand.

And the playtest you never have time to do now runs by machine. npm run check:balance plays 1,000 duels, fuzzes 250 more, runs the bot ladder, and answers green or red against thresholds in a file — no table to read. It checks eleven things: soft-locks, stalls, corrupt states, draws, first-player advantage, duel length, dead opening hands, all three difficulty matchups, and whether an early lead simply decides the game. It also names the over- and under-performing cards inside each mana cost, and diffs every run against the last one so you can tell whether a balance change actually helped or just moved the problem. Every one of those checks was proven by deliberately breaking the game and watching that check go red.

done  Card faces are drawn from data now, not baked

Every face used to be a finished 600×840 image rendered outside this project, and that single fact caused three separate problems. 55 of the 80 Battlecries still had the word Ongoing printed on them. A buffed or damaged stat had to be pasted over the picture as a coloured pill, because a printed number cannot change. And the hover preview needed a second copy of the effect text underneath it, because the text on the image was too small to read and might be out of date.

The card is DOM now. Banner, mana and ATK/HP gems, camp and alignment rails, description, flavour and origin are all drawn from data/cards.csv at render time, inside the same 750×1050 design space the print template uses. Buffing a card is editing one line of that file: no re-render, no re-import. A changed stat recolours its own gem — green up, red down — instead of growing a pill on top of it, and the duplicate text panel under the preview is gone.

Small cards drop their text so the art can breathe; hover one and the full printed card appears. A baked image could never do either. The artwork half of the old 55-face debt is closed for good: a card can no longer disagree with the file it came from. What survives is the wording inside the file — that is item 2 above, and it is now a one-word edit per card instead of a re-render.

And the wording is now honest too. 67 cards printed a timing that was not their own — 59 Battlecries still opening with “Ongoing:” or “Passive:”, and 8 more labelled the wrong way round in both directions. Every one now matches what the engine actually does: Battlecries carry no timing word, Ongoing engines say Ongoing:, passives say Passive:, and the keyword prefixes were dropped because keywords already have their own column and their own badge. Two also lied about duration and were reworded against the engine source (Homelander’s one-off +5/+5, and Furious Five’s shield, which has no “this turn” limit).

Every effect now states its own timing. On top of the 67 that were mislabelled, the last 19 that carried no timing word at all — 12 Ongoing engines and 7 passives reading like one-shots — were labelled, and the 16 cards whose Taunt, Divine Shield or Chained was never written down anywhere now say so. Nothing in the roster is silent about what it does.

This document stopped carrying pictures. Its roster held 196 baked card faces as embedded images, about 1 MB of them, and they were the last thing here still showing the old wording. They are gone: each entry is now a live text tile — name, cost, stats, camp, alignment and the real effect text, straight from data/cards.csv. The file went from 1.13 MB to 95 KB and can no longer disagree with the game. To see the artwork, open the game.
